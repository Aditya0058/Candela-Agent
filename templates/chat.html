At its core, the double-slit experiment demonstrates that tiny particles like electrons can behave like both **particles** and **waves**.

Imagine you're firing tiny marbles at a wall with two vertical slits:

  • 01 If they were purely particles, you'd see two stripes on the back wall where the marbles hit.
  • 02 However, when we fire electrons, they create an **interference pattern**—many stripes of varying intensity.
  • 03 This happens because the electron's "wave of probability" passes through both slits at once and interferes with itself.

def electron_position(x, t):
    # Simulating a basic wave function interference
    psi_1 = wave_function(x - slit_a, t)
    psi_2 = wave_function(x - slit_b, t)
    
    # The probability is the square of the sum of amplitudes
    probability = abs(psi_1 + psi_2)**2
    return probability

The strangest part? If we try to "watch" which slit the electron goes through, it stops acting like a wave and reverts to behaving like a simple particle. The act of observation changes reality.
